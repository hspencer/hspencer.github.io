Climbing the Sacred Mountain of Doctoral Legitimacy

14 de diciembre de 2025

Notas

  1. Format 1 is the conventional research doctorate. Knowledge is produced primarily through analytical argumentation, empirical data, and theoretical synthesis, and is presented as a single, extended written thesis. Practice may inform the research, but it is not itself the primary site of knowledge production.
    Format 2 is practice-informed. Creative or professional practice plays an important role in shaping the inquiry, but the main contribution to knowledge is still articulated through a substantial written thesis. Practice supports the research rather than constituting it.
    Format 3, which I am undertaking, is practice-oriented (often called practice-led or practice-based). Here, practice is not illustrative or auxiliary; it is the central means through which knowledge is generated. Artefacts, systems, prototypes, performances, or designed interventions are treated as epistemic objects. The written component—the exegesis—does not replace the practice, nor does it attempt to fully translate it into text. Instead, it situates, reflects on, and critically articulates what the practice produces as knowledge.
    This format is particularly relevant for architecture, design, and the arts, where understanding often emerges through making, iteration, encounter, and failure. In this sense, Format 3 formalises something many of us have already been doing for years: treating practice as a legitimate form of inquiry rather than as a prelude to real research.
    This format does not yet exist in Chile in any formal sense, and its institutional recognition was a decisive reason for undertaking my doctorate in Aotearoa New Zealand. I was drawn by the possibility of working within a doctoral structure that acknowledged practice as epistemically productive in its own right, and by the prospect of bringing that model back into conversations about doctoral education in Valparaíso. What I did not fully anticipate, however, was the extent to which this promising structure is still underwritten by a strong epistemic canon—one that continues to shape what counts as focus, rigour, and legitimacy. Recognising that canon has become part of the work itself, and it is a tension I now carry consciously as I think about how such a format might be adapted, rather than imported wholesale, into the design of future doctoral programmes in Chile. ↩

From Valparaíso to Tāmaki Makaurau

I am currently undertaking a practice-led doctorate1 at Auckland University of Technology (AUT), in Aotearoa New Zealand.

From the perspective of my academic trajectory in Chile, this step is less a personal milestone than a structural necessity. In my school, postgraduate education and research have been consolidating over time, and doctoral participation matters: it shapes supervision capacity, research culture, and institutional responsibility. I write from within that system, with the intention of inhabiting it fully and contributing to it as a future supervisor.

This doctorate also sits within a longer institutional relationship. Some years ago, our faculties initiated a collaboration grounded in the recognition of shared conditions as schools of the so-called Global South. The aim was not to imitate distant centres of authority, but to cultivate closer references, shared concerns, and a strong commitment to making as a mode of inquiry. In architecture and design, knowledge often emerges in the encounter with a work-in-progress: with materials, constraints, people, and contexts. That encounter has an irreducible character. It produces outcomes that cannot be fully anticipated, because the work is shaped by contingencies and negotiations that exceed any single plan.

Both New Zealand and Chile operate, culturally and academically, with what I would describe as an island mindset. Despite strong local traditions of practice and thought, there is a persistent tendency to seek external validation—from standards, frameworks, and evaluative models perceived as more authoritative because they originate in larger or more central academic systems.

This tendency produces a paradox. Even when we are critical of imported frameworks, we can end up defending them with greater rigidity than their original proponents. The borrowed model becomes something to protect rather than something to work with. Precision hardens into narrowness. Criteria become orthodoxy. What began as a useful structure turns into a gatekeeping device.

The promise of practice-led research is straightforward: practice is not an illustration of theory; it is a primary site where knowledge is generated, tested, revised, and made communicable. The artefact (or system, or body of work) carries epistemic weight, while the exegesis clarifies how that work constitutes research within a university context.

In my reading, this format responds to a long-standing issue in design education. Projective disciplines already operate with forms of rigour that do not resemble laboratory protocols. They organise inquiry through iteration, critique, rehearsal, prototyping, and situated judgement. Method, in this sense, is often a living arrangement rather than a fixed procedure. When a university recognises practice-led research, it is making room—at least in principle—for that arrangement to count as doctoral work.

Yet the university also inherits expectations from other research traditions. It expects a research question that can be stated cleanly; a rationale that can be summarised quickly; a method that can be described as a stable sequence; an output that can be audited under examination conditions. These expectations can coexist with practice-led inquiry, but the relationship is tense. The tension becomes visible when a project thinks in branching forms, or when its writing connects ideas relationally, sometimes poetically.

In my first evaluated doctoral milestone, my project was perceived as too diffuse, too broad, and too ambitious. The critique focused less on the topic itself than on questions of viability and focus. I was told, in effect, that the project did not yet fit the shape of an examinable doctorate. I have kept those materials public as a record of this negotiation2.

One phrase has stayed with me: the writing and framing needed to be “easy to explain to a teenager.” I understand the motive. In an institutional environment in which examiners must compare projects across disciplines, maximal communicability serves as a safeguard. It protects the system’s capacity to evaluate, and it protects the candidate from drifting into an unfinishable thesis.

But this raises a difficult question. When communicability becomes the dominant criterion, what happens to forms of thinking that operate through complexity, resonance, and layered connections? What happens to rhetorical traditions—such as many Latin American ones—where thought is often carried by metaphor, associative movement, and a tolerance for ambiguity understood as a cognitive resource rather than a failure of discipline?

Precision as a gate, and “narrowness” as a metaphor

I have come to see that what is called “precision” in doctoral framing often functions as a rhetorical gate. It demands early closure: a narrowing of the field until the inquiry fits a predetermined container. This can be useful. It forces commitments, reduces risk, and establishes a contract between the candidate and the examiner.

The difficulty is that “narrow and deep” is itself a metaphor, and metaphors shape what institutions recognise as valid knowledge. When narrowness is treated as the default sign of seriousness, it becomes an epistemic preference. It privileges linear thought, stable variables, and arguments that move in a single direction. It is a disadvantage of thinking that works by mapping relations, holding tensions, and allowing questions to emerge through practice.

Design often proceeds by cultivating a field of relations before deciding where to cut. It is diagrammatic. It notices parallelisms. It moves sideways. In this sense, a practice-led doctorate faces a paradox: the university invites practice as inquiry, yet still pressures the candidate to speak through a rhetoric that prunes the practice into a single trunk.

This is where the feeling of discrimination can become tangible. The issue is not simply language proficiency or stylistic preference. It is whether the institution can treat plural intellectual styles as legitimate, rather than as deviations to be corrected.

Inside and outside

I now think of my doctoral work as having two faces.

Inside the project is where knowledge is generated: coding, prototyping, failing, revising, negotiating meaning with participants, confronting the resistance of materials and constraints, and gradually building an artefact that can be used and criticised. This is where practice-led research does what it claims.

Outside the project is how that work must pass through a narrow opening: a research question in one sentence; a diagram; a bounded method; a claim that can be repeated quickly. This outside version is not dishonest, but it is reductive by design. It compresses lived inquiry into a form that can be examined efficiently.

The risk is that the outside version becomes the “real” one, because it is what committees see, what forms accept, and what funding panels read. A doctoral structure can end up rewarding the performance of clarity over the work of inquiry.

Here I find a useful analogy in Asimov’s first law of robotics, which states that a robot must not harm a human being. I would adapt this as a supervisory principle: doctoral structure must not damage the project that gives the doctorate its reason to exist3.

The candidate has to push the project through the narrow opening of academic rhetoric. The supervisor cannot do this in the candidate’s place. What the supervisor can do is protect the spirit of the work while helping the candidate translate it into examinable language. Supervision, in this view, is a form of translation. It does not demand submission to a single intellectual style; it helps the work find a form the academy can read without forcing it to become what it is not.

If I imagine myself as a future supervisor, this is the role I want to inhabit: preventing worthwhile projects from collapsing under rhetorical compliance, and treating alternative intellectual styles as part of the research ecology rather than as errors.

I am not alone

These tensions are not idiosyncratic. They have names in the history of ideas.

Paul Feyerabend argued against the idea of a single privileged method, insisting that rigid methodological rules often misdescribe how knowledge is actually produced, and that plurality is not a defect but a condition of discovery. His stance is often caricatured as “anything goes”, but the deeper point is epistemic freedom: institutions should not convert one model of rationality into a monopoly4.

Donna Haraway criticised5 the fantasy of disembodied objectivity and proposed “situated knowledges”: claims become more accountable when their position, limits, and relations are stated, rather than hidden behind a universal voice. This supports forms of research writing that acknowledge location, responsibility, and partial perspective—features that practice-led work often carries naturally.

Boaventura de Sousa Santos describes “epistemicide” and argues for cognitive justice6: the idea that there can be no social justice without plurality in what counts as knowledge. From this angle, the demand for a single doctoral rhetoric is not neutral; it can suppress other ways of knowing that carry different histories and needs.

Gloria Anzaldúa wrote from the borderlands—linguistic, cultural, and intellectual—and treated hybrid writing as a legitimate way of doing theory7. Her work matters here because it shows that clarity does not require flattening voice, and that intellectual precision can coexist with metaphor, narrative, and bilingual thought.

Walter Mignolo speaks of epistemic disobedience8: refusing to accept that the only valid thinking is the thinking authorised by Western academic canons. Whether one agrees with his entire programme or not, his work names a problem that many researchers experience when their intellectual style comes from elsewhere.

Arturo Escobar’s idea of the pluriverse—“a world where many worlds fit”—extends this logic to design9. If design is a practice of world-making, then doctoral education in design should not operate as a one-world university, demanding that all inquiry conform to a single rhetorical architecture.

Catherine Walsh argues for interculturality as an epistemic project, not as a token gesture10. In her terms, the academy’s task is to create conditions for dialogue between knowledges, rather than absorbing difference into a single evaluative grammar.

Closer to my immediate institutional context, Welby Ings writes about invisible intelligence: forms of intelligence that institutions often recognise only after they have already failed the people who carried them11.

Taken together, these voices do not eliminate the need for standards. They remind us that standards are historical and political, and that a university which confuses one epistemic style with epistemic virtue risks becoming an instrument of exclusion.

Higher education now faces additional pressure. AI intensifies existing strains. It makes rhetorical fluency easier to simulate, weakening simple proxies such as “easy to explain to a teenager.” In this context, clarity remains valuable, but it is no longer sufficient.

What matters more is judgment: knowing where a project is trying to go, why that direction is justified, and how responsibility is maintained as the work moves through situated constraints. Much of this intelligence resides beyond text, in decisions about form, sequencing, exclusion, and timing—decisions shaped by practice and exposure to consequences.

If universities respond by tightening templates further, they may protect administrative legibility at the expense of intellectual purpose. A different response is possible: protecting what cannot be easily automated—situated inquiry, accountable judgement, and knowledge that emerges through practice and encounter.

My position is not anti-academic. It is an argument for plurality within the academy, especially in doctoral education for design and the arts. A practice-led doctorate should not be a polite exception that still has to speak as if it were a conventional thesis. It should be a legitimate site of epistemic diversity.

I am learning to frame my work in a narrower, examinable way, because I want to finish the doctorate with integrity. At the same time, I am learning what this framing is for. In an AI-saturated context, rhetorical fluency is no longer a reliable proxy for intellectual quality. The doctoral format must justify itself by foregrounding what is harder to simulate: traceable judgement, defensible exclusions, and inquiry that remains accountable to practice.

That is the kind of doctoral culture I want to contribute to: disciplined, examinable, and plural. If the university is to remain a place where knowledge is produced rather than reproduced, plurality must function as an operating condition, not a courtesy.
