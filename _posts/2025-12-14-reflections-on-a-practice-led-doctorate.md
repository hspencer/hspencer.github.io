#### From Valparaíso to Tāmaki Makaurau

I am currently undertaking a practice-led doctorate (Format 3) at Auckland University of Technology (AUT), in Aotearoa New Zealand. From the perspective of my academic trajectory in Chile, this step is less a personal milestone than a structural necessity. In my school we have been consolidating postgraduate education and research, and doctoral-level participation matters: it shapes supervision capacity, research culture, and institutional responsibility. I am writing this as someone who intends to inhabit this system fully, and to contribute to it as a future supervisor.  

This pathway also sits within a longer institutional relationship. A few years ago, our faculties began a collaboration building ties as schools of the so-called Global South. The point was not to imitate distant centres of authority, but to cultivate closer references, shared concerns, and an orientation towards making as a mode of inquiry. In architecture and design, knowledge often emerges in the encounter with a work-in-progress, with materials, constraints, people, and contexts. That encounter has an irreducible character: it produces outcomes that cannot be fully anticipated, because the work is shaped by contingencies and negotiations that exceed any single plan. This is part of what makes projective practice a knowledge practice rather than a decorative addition to “real” research.

The promise of practice-led research is straightforward: practice is not an illustration of theory, it is a primary site where knowledge is generated, tested, revised, and made communicable. The artefact (or system, or body of work) carries epistemic weight, while the exegesis clarifies how that work constitutes research within a university.  

In my reading, this format responds to a long-standing problem in design education. Projective disciplines already operate with forms of rigour that do not resemble laboratory protocols. They organise inquiry through iteration, critique, rehearsal, prototyping, and situated judgement. The “method” is often a living arrangement rather than a fixed procedure. When a university recognises practice-led research, it is making room—at least in principle—for that arrangement to count as doctoral work.  

And yet, the university still inherits expectations that come from other traditions. It expects a research question that can be stated cleanly; a rationale that can be summarised quickly; a method that can be described in a stable sequence; an output that can be audited under exam conditions. These expectations can coexist with practice-led inquiry, but the relationship is tense. The tension becomes visible when a project thinks in branching forms, or when its writing connects ideas in a relational, sometimes poetic mode.

In my first evaluated doctoral milestone, my project was perceived as too diffuse, too broad, and too ambitious. The critique was not primarily about my topic, but about viability and focus. I was told, in effect, that the project did not yet fit the shape of an examinable doctorate. I keep those materials public as a record of this negotiation((PGR9 materials (archived): [https://github.com/hspencer/cc](https://github.com/hspencer/cc) and the [initial project text](https://github.com/hspencer/cc/blob/main/mediafranca.md).)).

One phrase has stayed with me: the writing and framing needed to be “easy to explain to a teenager”. I understand the motive. In an institutional environment where examiners must compare projects across disciplines, maximal communicability becomes a safety mechanism. It protects the system’s capacity to evaluate, and it protects the candidate from drifting into an unfinishable thesis.

But it also raises a difficult question: when communicability becomes the dominant criterion, what happens to kinds of thinking that operate through complexity, resonance, and layered connections? What happens to forms of writing that come from other rhetorical traditions, including Latin American ones, where thought is often carried by metaphor, by associative movement, and by a certain tolerance for ambiguity as a cognitive resource rather than a failure of discipline?

#### Precision as a gate, and “narrowness” as a metaphor

I have come to see that what is called “precision” in doctoral framing often functions as a rhetorical gate. It is a demand for early closure: a narrowing of the field until the inquiry fits a predetermined container. This can be useful. It forces commitments. It reduces risk. It creates a contract between candidate and examiner.  

The problem is that “narrow and deep” is also a metaphor, and metaphors shape what institutions can recognise as valid knowledge. When narrowness is treated as the default sign of seriousness, it becomes a form of epistemic preference. It privileges linear thought, stable variables, and arguments that move in a single direction. It disadvantages thinking that works by mapping relations, holding tensions, and allowing the work to disclose its own questions through practice.

Design often proceeds by cultivating a field of relations before deciding where to cut. It is diagrammatic. It notices parallelisms. It moves sideways. In that sense, a practice-led doctorate faces a paradox: the university invites practice as inquiry, but still pressures the candidate to speak through a rhetoric that prunes the practice into a single trunk.

This is where the feeling of discrimination can become real. The issue is not only language proficiency or genre preference. It is whether the institution can treat plural intellectual styles as legitimate, rather than as deviations to be corrected.

##### inside and outside

I now describe my doctoral work as having two faces.

**Inside the project** is where the knowledge is generated: coding, prototyping, failing, revising, negotiating meaning with participants, confronting the resistance of materials and constraints, and slowly building an artefact that can be used and criticised. This is where practice-led research does what it claims.

**Outside the project** is how the project must pass through a narrow opening: a research question in one sentence; a diagram; a bounded method; a claim that can be repeated at speed. This outside version is not dishonest, but it is reductive by design. It compresses lived inquiry into a form that can be examined efficiently.

The risk is that the outside version becomes the “real” one, because it is what committees see, what forms accept, and what funding panels read. A doctoral structure can end up rewarding the performance of clarity over the work of inquiry.

Here I find a useful analogy: Asimov’s first law of robotics, which states that a robot must not harm a human being. I would adapt this as a supervisory principle: doctoral structure must not damage the project that gives the doctorate its reason to exist((Isaac Asimov, “Three Laws of Robotics” (popularised across multiple texts; a concise reference point is *I, Robot*, 1950).)).

The candidate has to push the project through the narrow opening of academic rhetoric. The supervisor cannot do that in the candidate’s place. But the supervisor can do something else: protect the spirit of the work while helping the candidate translate it into examinable language. Supervision, in this view, is a translation practice. It does not demand submission to a single intellectual style (“bend the knee”); it helps the work find a form that the academy can read without forcing the work to become what it is not.

If I picture myself as a future supervisor, this is the role I want: to prevent worthwhile projects from collapsing under rhetorical compliance, and to treat alternative intellectual styles as part of the research ecology, not as errors.

### I am not alone

These tensions are not idiosyncratic. They have names in the history of ideas.

Paul Feyerabend argued against the idea of a single privileged method, insisting that rigid methodological rules often misdescribe how knowledge is actually produced, and that plurality is not a defect but a condition of discovery. His stance is often caricatured as “anything goes”, but the deeper point is epistemic freedom: institutions should not convert one model of rationality into a monopoly((Paul Feyerabend, *Against Method* (1975; later editions).)).

Donna Haraway criticised((Donna Haraway, “Situated Knowledges: The Science Question in Feminism and the Privilege of Partial Perspective” (1988).)) the fantasy of disembodied objectivity and proposed “situated knowledges”: claims become more accountable when their position, limits, and relations are stated, rather than hidden behind a universal voice. This supports forms of research writing that acknowledge location, responsibility, and partial perspective—features that practice-led work often carries naturally.

Boaventura de Sousa Santos describes “epistemicide” and argues for cognitive justice((Boaventura de Sousa Santos, *Epistemologies of the South* (various essays; see also his work on “cognitive justice” and “epistemicide”).)): the idea that there can be no social justice without plurality in what counts as knowledge. From this angle, the demand for a single doctoral rhetoric is not neutral; it can suppress other ways of knowing that carry different histories and needs.

Gloria Anzaldúa wrote from the borderlands—linguistic, cultural, and intellectual—and treated hybrid writing as a legitimate way of doing theory((Gloria Anzaldúa, *Borderlands/La Frontera: The New Mestiza* (1987).)). Her work matters here because it shows that clarity does not require flattening voice, and that intellectual precision can coexist with metaphor, narrative, and bilingual thought.

Walter Mignolo speaks of epistemic disobedience((Walter D. Mignolo, work on “epistemic disobedience” and “border thinking” (e.g., essays from 2009–2011 onwards).)): refusing to accept that the only valid thinking is the thinking authorised by Western academic canons. Whether one agrees with his entire programme or not, his work names a problem that many researchers experience when their intellectual style comes from elsewhere.

Arturo Escobar’s idea of the pluriverse—“a world where many worlds fit”—extends this logic to design((Arturo Escobar, *Designs for the Pluriverse* (2018).)). If design is a practice of world-making, then doctoral education in design should not operate as a one-world university, demanding that all inquiry conform to a single rhetorical architecture.

Catherine Walsh argues for interculturality as an epistemic project, not as a token gesture((Catherine Walsh, essays on interculturality and decoloniality (mid-2000s onwards).)). In her terms, the academy’s task is to create conditions for dialogue between knowledges, rather than absorbing difference into a single evaluative grammar. 

And closer to my immediate institutional context, Welby Ings writes about “invisible intelligence”((Welby Ings, “Invisible intelligence” (e-tangata): https://e-tangata.co.nz/reflections/invisible-intelligence/ and related writing in his work on education and assessment.)): forms of intelligence that institutions can perceive only after they have already failed the person who carried them. His reflections describe a sorrowful contradiction—recognition arriving too late because the system could not accommodate difference at the time it mattered. That contradiction is an educational warning, including for doctoral education.

Taken together, these voices do not remove the need for standards. They do something else: they remind us that standards are political and historical, and that a university that confuses one epistemic style with epistemic virtue risks becoming an instrument of exclusion.

There is a further pressure now: higher education is under visible strain—financially, institutionally, and epistemically. AI intensifies that strain. It challenges universities in at least three ways.

First, it destabilises what counts as individual production, and therefore what counts as assessment. Second, it raises the value of tacit judgement: the ability to evaluate, to interpret, to situate, and to take responsibility for a claim. Third, it makes rhetorical fluency easier to simulate. “Easy to explain to a teenager” will become an even less reliable proxy for intellectual quality when polished simplifications can be generated on demand.

If universities respond by tightening templates further—more standardisation, more audit, more rhetorical gatekeeping—they may protect administrative legibility at the expense of intellectual purpose. The risk is a university that becomes a factory of compliant outputs, while the actual work of thought migrates elsewhere.

A different response is possible. In the era of AI, universities can justify themselves by protecting what cannot be automated in any simple way: situated inquiry, responsible judgement, and forms of knowledge that arise through practice, collaboration, and lived encounter. Practice-led research belongs here. It treats making as inquiry, and inquiry as accountable relation.

My position is not anti-academic. It is an argument for plurality inside the academy, especially in doctoral education for design and the arts. A practice-led doctorate should not be a polite exception that still has to speak as if it were a conventional thesis. It should be a legitimate site of epistemic diversity: a place where different kinds of intelligence can be recognised while they are still alive, not only after the institution has already filtered them out.

I am learning to frame my work in a narrower, examinable way, because I want to finish the doctorate and to do so with integrity. But I am also learning something about supervision: the supervisor’s work is not to domesticate a project into monoculture. It is to help the academy find the project’s language, while guarding what the project already knows in practice.

That is the kind of doctoral culture I want to contribute to: disciplined, examinable, and plural. If the university is to remain a place where knowledge is produced rather than reproduced, it needs that plurality as a condition of survival.
